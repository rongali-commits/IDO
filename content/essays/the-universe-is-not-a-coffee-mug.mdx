The easiest version of the cosmological argument is also the weakest: everything has a cause; the universe is a thing; therefore somebody caused the universe.

A schoolchild can spot the loose wire. Who caused the somebody?

That reply is satisfying, and usually inadequate. Serious defenders of cosmological arguments do not claim that absolutely everything must have a cause. They argue that whatever *begins* to exist has a cause, or that contingent things require an explanation while a necessary reality does not. If I want to disagree—and I do—I should disagree with the argument they actually make.

The title of this essay is my reminder. A coffee mug is the sort of object whose origin we understand because it appears within an already functioning world. Clay, labour, heat, machinery, money, transport, and time precede it. The universe is not one more manufactured item placed beside the mug. It is the total setting in which mugs, makers, succession, physical law, and the word *before* become possible.

Perhaps that setting has a cause. The analogy does not establish one.

## Two arguments that should not be blurred together

The first important distinction is between the *kalām* cosmological argument and an argument from contingency. They belong to the same family, but they do different work.

In its familiar modern form, the kalām argument says:

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

The contingency argument starts elsewhere. Things in the world could have failed to exist. They depend on conditions beyond themselves. Even if the universe had no first moment, a defender may ask why this entire collection of contingent reality exists at all. The proposed answer is a necessary being or necessary ground—something whose non-existence is impossible and which does not borrow existence from anything else.

Neither argument says, “Everything has a cause, except my preferred exception.” That caricature avoids the hard part. The real dispute is whether the proposed categories—begun and unbegun, contingent and necessary—are clear at the scale of reality as a whole, and whether a divine mind is the best terminus once those categories are accepted.

I remain unconvinced, but not because the arguments are foolish. They are powerful partly because they expose an irritation that naturalism cannot rub away: there is something rather than nothing, and every attempted explanation seems to stand on something it did not explain.

## Causes normally operate inside time

Suppose I knock the mug from my desk. The fall precedes the impact; my careless hand precedes the fall. Cause and effect are arranged in time, and both occur within a shared physical order. Even when causation becomes difficult—at the quantum scale, for example—the question is still framed by a mathematical theory describing states and relations.

Now consider a cause of spacetime itself. What could *precede* time without “precede” becoming a misleading word? A theologian may answer that the cause is timeless and that temporal effects can depend on a non-temporal reality. That position is coherent enough to discuss, but it is no longer continuous with the examples used to make causation feel obvious. We have moved from falling mugs to a metaphysical relation unlike any cause we observe.

The move may be legitimate. It cannot be smuggled through as common sense.

There is a second difficulty. Causes pick out changes: a seed germinates, a star collapses, a glass breaks. If there was no earlier physical state from which the universe changed, “cause” may mean explanation or dependence rather than an event that produced another event. That broader meaning has philosophical uses, though it also changes what has to be proved.

At this point the dispute is not between science and superstition. It is between rival accounts of what an explanation must eventually look like.

## The Big Bang is not a creation scene

Popular pictures of the Big Bang cause trouble. They show a bright point exploding into a surrounding darkness, which quietly supplies the very space the theory is meant to describe. People then ask what lit the point.

Modern cosmology does not describe an explosion at one location in a pre-existing room. It describes an early hot, dense state and the expansion of space. The evidence—cosmic expansion, the cosmic microwave background, and primordial element abundances—supports that history extraordinarily well. It does not follow that physics has reconstructed an absolute beginning from nothing.

NASA's overview of the universe says the observable story reaches an early period of inflation, while also acknowledging that scientists do not know what came before inflation or what powered it. That is a real boundary in present knowledge, not an invitation to insert any answer we like. “We do not know” is neither proof of God nor proof that a universe emerged uncaused.

The second premise of the kalām argument therefore cannot be settled by pointing at a standard Big Bang diagram. Some cosmological models contain a finite past; others explore bounces, cycles, quantum beginnings, or forms of past extension. Whether those models succeed is a scientific question. Whether a finite physical description amounts to an absolute metaphysical beginning is another.

I distrust arguments that use the prestige of cosmology while skipping this difference. Science should be allowed to be incomplete without being recruited as a witness for either side.

## Quantum mechanics does not give us free universes

Sceptics sometimes answer the first kalām premise by announcing that quantum events occur without causes. The sentence is repeated so often that it has begun to sound like a laboratory result. It is not that simple.

Quantum theory predicts probabilities with astonishing accuracy, but what the theory says reality is doing remains contested. On some interpretations, an event lacks a determining prior cause. On others, the evolution is deterministic, the apparent randomness reflects branching worlds, or additional variables restore a different account of dependence. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's survey of quantum mechanics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm/) does not present a single agreed metaphysics because physicists and philosophers do not have one.

Nor is the quantum vacuum the philosophical nothing. It is described by fields, rules, symmetries, and possible states. Saying that a universe can arise from a quantum condition may be a profound physical claim, but it has not answered why that condition and those rules obtain.

This cuts both ways. Quantum uncertainty does not refute every causal principle. It also does not rescue the idea that our everyday expectation of a cause can simply be extended to the universe. The physics is stranger than either slogan.

## The necessary-being proposal moves the question

The contingency argument avoids dependence on a first moment. Even an eternal universe, it says, might still need a sufficient reason for existing rather than not existing. A necessary being ends the regress because its existence is not contingent.

Here my resistance becomes sharper. I can understand the definition of a necessary being, but a definition is not yet a discovery. Why think the necessary foundation is a mind? Why suppose it has intentions, knowledge, moral concern, or any connection to a particular religion? Moving from “contingent reality may require a ground” to “that ground is a personal creator” needs several arguments, not a change of vocabulary.

There is also a question of explanatory cost. A timeless, immaterial mind capable of grounding every physical fact is not a modest posit. It may be simple in a theologian's technical sense—without parts, perhaps—but conceptually it carries immense weight. If the universe cannot be a brute fact because that would leave existence unexplained, declaring a necessary consciousness can feel less like completing the explanation than choosing a more dignified place to stop.

The theist can return the pressure. Treating physical reality as brute is also a decision to stop. Saying “the universe just exists” does not illuminate why its laws have this structure, why it permits complexity, or why there is a reality capable of being described at all.

That criticism lands. Naturalism has no right to pretend otherwise.

## Explanations always threaten to run out

Imagine that every fact must be explained by another fact. We immediately face a regress. Perhaps the chain is infinite. Perhaps it closes into a loop. Perhaps it ends in a necessary being, a necessary mathematical structure, a fundamental physical state, or a brute fact that admits no further explanation.

Each option violates an expectation. An infinite regress never supplies a final ground. A loop appears to explain its members by one another. Necessity can look like a label attached where questions become difficult. A brute fact refuses the demand that began the inquiry.

I do not know which discomfort reality chooses.

That admission matters because philosophical debates often reward confidence more quickly than accuracy. Believers can speak as if the cosmological argument delivers a creator with a personality and a moral programme. Atheists can speak as if invoking quantum mechanics makes existence ordinary. Both sides cross gaps their premises have not closed.

The better question may be not “What caused the universe?” but “What kind of explanation, if any, could apply to reality as a whole?” A causal story is one candidate. A statement of necessity is another. Some philosophers think the demand itself is misplaced: explanations operate within the world and need not explain the existence of the entire explanatory order.

I am drawn to that last position, though “drawn” is doing honest work here. It expresses a philosophical preference, not a measurement. I suspect that our need for a terminating answer was trained by problems that have edges, and reality may not owe us the same shape.

## What the Webb image leaves open

Webb's First Deep Field contains thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky roughly the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length. The foreground cluster appears as it was 4.6 billion years ago, and its mass magnifies still more distant galaxies behind it. The image is evidence, not decoration: mass bends light, distant galaxies record earlier cosmic periods, and patient engineering can turn photons into history.

It tells us nothing directly about why there is a universe. That silence is useful.

The image separates two kinds of wonder that are too often mixed. One is earned by finding out how things work. It builds instruments, tests predictions, corrects errors, and accepts that a cherished model may fail. The other begins where explanation thins out and asks why any lawful reality is present to investigate.

Religion does not own the second kind. Science does not abolish it.

I still think the coffee-mug intuition misleads us. Manufactured objects train us to expect makers, while the universe is not known to belong to that class. Yet the failure of an analogy does not establish that reality is self-explanatory, eternal, accidental, or uncaused. It only removes one shortcut.

What remains is a choice among strange possibilities, with no ordinary experience large enough to act as referee. We should keep asking—but the next useful answer will have to do more than make the mystery sound familiar.
