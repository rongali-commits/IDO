On 1 July 2024, three new criminal laws came into force in India. One of them, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, replaced the Indian Penal Code of 1860. The old code had survived the Raj, independence, Partition, the adoption of the Constitution, wars, elections, liberalisation, and seven and a half decades of argument about what a republic should be.

Its replacement was presented as a break with colonial thinking. The claim was disputed, as legal claims tend to be, but the date supplied a useful measurement. An empire can lose the power to appoint a single official and still remain present in the forms through which millions of people are policed, tried, educated, counted, and governed.

This is what I mean when I say the British Empire did not simply vanish. I do not mean that Britain secretly commands its former colonies. It plainly does not. I mean that formal independence and institutional inheritance happen on different clocks.

That distinction is easy to miss because imperial history is usually drawn as territory. The map turns pink, then the pink recedes. Seen that way, decolonisation resembles an eraser passing over a page. But states are not made only of land. They are also filing systems, legal categories, railway alignments, languages of administration, borders, entrance examinations, police procedures, commercial routes, and assumptions about what an official is entitled to know.

Those things are not erased by lowering a flag.

## I am writing this in English

The most immediate imperial inheritance is sitting in this sentence. I am an Indian writer publishing an essay in English about the afterlife of British power. That choice is mine; nobody in London assigned it. Yet it was not made on an empty field.

English gives an Indian writer access to readers in Bengaluru, Lagos, Toronto, Melbourne, and London without asking all of them to share another language. It is used in Indian courts, universities, technology companies, Parliament, journalism, and a great deal of ordinary urban life. The Constitution itself preserves a role for English in the higher judiciary: Article 348 provides for English in the Supreme Court and High Courts, subject to the exceptions and legislative powers set out there.

Calling English merely a foreign imposition is therefore inadequate. Calling it a neutral global convenience is equally evasive. It arrived in its modern institutional role through conquest, was made valuable by an empire, and was then seized, adapted, and enlarged by people the empire had ruled. Indians have used it to administer the state, oppose the state, write novels Britain could not have produced, build companies, and argue with one another.

A language can carry the history of domination without remaining the property of the former ruler. That is uncomfortable, but it is also more interesting than a simple story of contamination.

English has become infrastructure far beyond the old empire. International aviation offers an unusually literal example. The International Civil Aviation Organization requires pilots and air traffic controllers working in specified international operations to demonstrate language proficiency; English must be available for communications at designated airports and routes. This is partly the legacy of British and American power, partly the result of path dependence, and partly a practical answer to the danger of hundreds of incompatible cockpit languages.

Once a common standard is widely adopted, abandoning it can be more expensive than remembering why it won.

## Law outlives its authors

The Indian Penal Code was drafted under British rule, chiefly associated with Thomas Babington Macaulay's first Law Commission, and enacted after the uprising of 1857. It was not an ornamental relic. It defined offences and organised punishment for generations of Indians.

India revised it repeatedly. Courts interpreted it through a democratic Constitution. Legislatures amended it. Citizens challenged its provisions. The code that existed in 2023 was not a sealed Victorian document accidentally left in a cupboard. It had acquired an Indian legal history, including decisions that limited or removed some of its worst features.

That makes the 2024 replacement more revealing, not less. Institutional inheritance is rarely a choice between keeping a perfectly British object and replacing it with a perfectly indigenous one. A legal system is modified while it is being used. New principles are attached to old procedures. Judges trained in inherited forms produce judgments hostile to colonial values. Police powers written for rulers are defended by elected governments.

The [Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/handle/123456789/20062) formally repeals the Penal Code, but a new title does not by itself settle whether the underlying relationship between citizen and state has changed. That judgment belongs to the text, its enforcement, and the courts. Decolonisation by renaming is easy. Decolonisation of power is slower and can embarrass every political party.

## There was never one British colonial experience

Any argument about imperial residue becomes dishonest if it treats the empire as a single machine producing identical results.

British rule in India depended on a small foreign elite, local intermediaries, princely states, an army recruited largely on the subcontinent, and a dense bureaucracy. In Australia, settler colonialism dispossessed Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples while migration changed the demographic foundation of the country. In parts of Africa, borders joined communities that had little reason to share a state and divided others that did. Caribbean economies were shaped by slavery, plantations, and coerced labour long before emancipation.

The differences are not footnotes. They determine what survives.

A former administrative colony may inherit civil-service hierarchies and court language while its population remains overwhelmingly indigenous. A settler society may possess British-derived parliaments and common law while the more enduring fact is that the settlers stayed, occupied land, and became the majority. A trading port may retain commercial networks that connect it more strongly to distant markets than to its own interior.

This is why ancestry figures require care. In Australia's 2021 Census, 33.0 percent of people reported English ancestry, 9.5 percent Irish, and 8.6 percent Scottish. Those categories do not add up to a racial inventory: respondents could nominate up to two ancestries, “Australian” was also one of the most common answers, and identity cannot be reduced to genealogy. Still, the figures record a demographic consequence of empire that independence could not reverse. Britain did not need to govern Australia from Westminster for descendants of British and Irish settlement to shape its institutions and public memory.

The presence of that inheritance does not make modern Australians agents of Britain. It does make the phrase “the empire ended” incomplete unless we ask what, exactly, ended.

## Borders turn decisions into facts

Imperial borders are often discussed as if officials drew arbitrary lines once and departed. Some lines were indeed drawn with breathtaking ignorance or indifference. Others followed older provinces, rivers, commercial interests, military frontiers, bargains with local powers, or previous conquests. Nearly all acquired lives of their own.

After independence, a border inherited from empire becomes a dilemma. Moving it may answer one historical grievance and create three new ones. Keeping it may freeze an unjust arrangement, but it can also prevent a war. The Organisation of African Unity chose in 1964 to respect the borders existing at independence, not because every border was wise, but because a continent-wide renegotiation threatened chaos.

This is institutional inheritance at its harshest. A bad decision can become difficult to undo precisely because millions of people have built lives around it.

The same principle applies less dramatically to railways, property records, district boundaries, and administrative capitals. Infrastructure encourages later development to follow the paths already laid. A railway built to move raw material toward a colonial port may become essential to commuters a century later. Its origin tells us what it was built to do; its present use tells us what people made from it. Both facts matter.

## Inheritance is not obedience

There is a political temptation on both sides of this subject. Defenders of empire point to courts, railways, or English and treat later usefulness as payment for conquest. Nationalists identify something as colonial and assume its removal must be liberation. Neither position requires much thought.

An institution can be useful now and still have been imposed for exploitative reasons. It can have a colonial origin and later be transformed by democratic use. It can also be renamed while preserving the old concentration of power. History does not provide moral ownership certificates.

The [UK Parliament's own history of Parliament and empire](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/) records how legislation and parliamentary authority were entangled with colonial expansion. Yet parliamentary government was also adapted by former colonies and used to remove British rule. The institution did not contain one permanent politics. Its users changed what it could mean.

That is where the phrase *background noise* earns its place. The empire is easiest to recognise in a statue or a governor's residence because those objects announce themselves. Its deeper remnants often appear ordinary: the language on a form, the shape of a district, the robe in a courtroom, the route a train takes, the accent treated as educated, the archive that decides which names can be found.

Ordinariness does not mean harmlessness. It means the argument has moved from whether the empire is still ruling to how later societies distribute, revise, or defend what it left behind.

## What would count as finishing decolonisation?

There is no final ceremony that can make a country historically untouched. India could replace every statute written before 1947 and still use political ideas, administrative habits, and languages formed through centuries of exchange and conflict. Australia could become a republic and still face the question of land. Britain could return every disputed object in every museum and remain wealthy in ways connected to empire.

The sensible goal is not purity. Purity is impossible, and attempts to manufacture it usually produce another form of coercion. The goal is conscious ownership: knowing where an institution came from, asking whom it serves now, and changing it when inherited power is mistaken for common sense.

On 2 July 2024, the old Indian Penal Code was history and the police still had cases to investigate. Courts still needed procedure. Citizens still needed protection from the state as well as from one another. The difficult work began after the symbolic break, because a republic proves its independence in the way authority is exercised when nobody is watching a flag.

That question does not belong to Britain anymore. It belongs to us.
