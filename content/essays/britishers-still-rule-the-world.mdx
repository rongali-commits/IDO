You think the British Empire is dead.

As a system of formal rule, it largely is. India became independent in 1947. Colonies across Africa, Asia, the Caribbean, and the Pacific followed. Governors left, flags changed, and sovereign states began making their own choices.

But political independence does not erase history. At its peak in the early twentieth century, the empire governed roughly a quarter of the world's land and population. It left behind people, borders, languages, legal traditions, universities, civil services, sports, and ideas about how a state should work.

So the useful question is not whether Britain secretly still rules its former empire. It does not. The question is whether systems built under empire continue to shape the choices available today.

## Two Very Different Colonial Projects

Britain did not settle every territory in the same way.

In India and much of Africa, British officials and soldiers governed populations that remained overwhelmingly local. In Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and the future United States, large-scale settlement, disease, dispossession, and violence transformed the demographic order itself.

That distinction matters. A colony administered by a small foreign elite leaves a different legacy from a settler society in which newcomers and their descendants become a large share of the population. Both involve coercion, but their afterlives are not identical.

The [UK Parliament's history of Parliament and empire](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/) documents how parliamentary authority expanded alongside empire. The institutions that travelled with it later became local institutions—adapted, contested, and ultimately owned by independent societies.

## The Map Ancestry Cannot Draw Cleanly

It is tempting to turn this legacy into one dramatic number: what percentage of Australia, Canada, or New Zealand is “British”? The evidence is messier.

Australia's 2021 Census, for example, allowed people to report up to two ancestries. The most common responses were English (33.0%), Australian (29.9%), Irish (9.5%), and Scottish (8.6%). Those categories overlap, and “Australian” can carry several meanings. Adding them together would double-count people and manufacture a false precision.

The official [Australian Bureau of Statistics QuickStats](https://www.abs.gov.au/census/find-census-data/quickstats/2021/AUS) therefore tells us that British and Irish ancestry remains important—not that one simple percentage captures the country's identity.

Demography also changes. Migration from Asia, Europe, Africa, and elsewhere has made former settler colonies far more plural than they were a century ago. Indigenous peoples, whose sovereignty and survival colonial statistics often obscured, are not historical footnotes in somebody else's national story.

> The flags changed. The population changed too—but the institutions changed at a different speed.

## The Blueprint That Outlasted the Builders

The United States makes the distinction between ancestry and institutions especially clear.

Its population was transformed by forced migration and by successive waves of immigration from across the world. Yet English remained dominant, common-law reasoning remained foundational, and political debates continued to borrow from and rebel against British precedents.

That does not make the United States “still British.” Institutions do not belong forever to their inventors. Once adopted, they are revised by the people who use them. American constitutionalism is not Westminster government; it is a distinct system shaped partly through opposition to Britain.

The better metaphor is not a British house whose occupants never changed the walls. It is an old blueprint that generations of owners have extended, demolished, repaired, and argued over.

## The Language That Became Infrastructure

English is the empire's most visible global legacy, but even here the story is not one-way domination.

International aviation requires pilots and controllers involved in international operations to demonstrate English proficiency. The [International Civil Aviation Organization](https://www.icao.int/safety/lpr/Pages/Language-Proficiency-Requirements.aspx) treats that common language as a safety standard. English is also widely used in science, diplomacy, software, and international business.

Yet global English no longer belongs to Britain. Indian English, Nigerian English, Singapore English, American English, and countless local forms are not defective copies. Speakers have remade the language, filled it with their own histories, and used it to challenge the power that first spread it.

Influence survived. Control did not.

## India: Inheritance and Reinvention

India is a revealing case precisely because independence was real and the inherited systems were substantial.

The Indian Penal Code of 1860 remained in force for more than seven decades after independence. Parliament replaced it through the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, enacted in 2023 and brought into force on 1 July 2024. The official text and commencement details are available through [India Code](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/handle/123456789/20062?sam_handle=123456789/1362).

Other inheritances remain visible: common-law reasoning, parts of the civil service, parliamentary procedure, English-language higher education, railways, and cricket. But calling these simply “British rule by other means” erases what Indians did with them.

India rewrote its Constitution, expanded universal suffrage, reorganised states, developed its own jurisprudence, and turned cricket into an arena where India holds enormous commercial power. An inherited institution can be colonial in origin and postcolonial in ownership at the same time.

## What Decolonisation Changed

Decolonisation changed the most important fact: who possessed lawful political authority.

It did not instantly repair dispossession, redraw every arbitrary border, replace every statute, or remove the economic advantages accumulated under empire. Independence was a transfer of power, not a magical deletion of path dependence.

This is why two simplistic claims both fail:

- “The empire vanished, so it no longer matters” ignores durable institutions and inequalities.
- “The empire still rules exactly as before” denies the agency and sovereignty of formerly colonised peoples.

The truth is more demanding. Formal rule ended. Unequal relationships, cultural influence, and institutional inheritance persisted in different degrees. Each country negotiated that inheritance differently.

## The Uncomfortable Question

If a legal system, language, or university survives the government that introduced it, whose institution is it now?

Origin matters because it reveals whose interests a system was designed to serve. Present ownership matters because people can transform a system into something its founders never intended. Both facts can be true.

The British Empire did not discover a way to rule forever. It created structures that outlived it—and then lost control of what those structures became.

That may be the more interesting legacy. Power can disappear while its defaults remain. People can inherit those defaults without being trapped by them. Decolonisation is therefore not a single date in a textbook. It is the continuing work of deciding what to keep, what to repair, and what to build anew.
