In January 1945, an American Army photographer named W. Chichersky stood at the station in Hayange, France, photographing displaced Russian civilians as soldiers loaded them onto a train. The caption explains where they are and what the soldiers are doing, but it does not say what happened to the passengers.

If you gaze long enough at this photo, the caption could be improved upon. “Displaced,” after all, is not exactly a term one would use to describe a person living in a different neighbourhood than they used to. One could be displaced from one's job, from one's lover, from one's wedding, from one's tightly knit village. Millions of people had their lives interrupted and rearranged in a way that was beyond their control. It is this category of people and events that World War II touches on, besides all the people it killed.

It also raises an uncomfortable question about the children born because the war changed who survived, who met, and when couples were reunited. It is not a matter of the war making people better or worse, but of the sheer fragility of each person's existence being decided by the vagaries of chance.

## The fundamental biology of being one person versus another

Two people conceiving a child at different times will rarely have the exact same child, even if they are the same two people.

Even a small delay would almost certainly bring a different sperm into the event and, across a longer interval, perhaps a different egg as well. That is enough to produce a different person.

The difference is easier to see when the interval is large. A child conceived in 1939 and one conceived in 1945 would begin from different gametes, even if they had the same parents. The earlier child is a person who could not first be conceived in 1945, while the latter could not have been conceived six years earlier. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nonidentity-problem/) goes into detail about this non-identity problem. It usually concerns our moral duties to possible future people, but I think it also makes an important observation about how thin the veneer over individual existence is. Your family tree looks as sturdy as it does partly because there was only ever one sequence of circumstances that resulted in you being born.

## War is exceptionally good at disrupting schedules and timelines

When historians look at military events, they care about dates and distances, about numbers of casualties and resources, about treaties and diplomacy. When you look at a family tree, you care about similar things, but on a smaller scale. You care whether the train was cancelled, whether it took an extra month to reach its destination, whether letters were delivered or not, whether the factory hired new workers when its usual source of labour was unavailable.

Displacement was a serious problem throughout post-war Europe. The United Nations has records of millions of refugees scattered throughout Europe and hundreds of camps maintained by the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration. These camps were, first and foremost, places where people lived. People got married and divorced, made friends and enemies, and had children. The UN archives contain the records of the Tannenbaum family, who survived the Holocaust and lived in an UNRRA camp in Grugliasco, Italy, where their son Peter was born.

The point is not that the camps and dislocations led to a wave of new families being formed. Many people were separated, and whole communities were uprooted. We also cannot always say that one event was the decisive factor in a person being born, or not being born, because causes tend to be intertwined and inseparable. What we do know is that large-scale movements of people intersect with personal timelines in ways that rarely leave a trace. That is why Chichersky's photograph offers an unusual way to look into the reproductive history of the twentieth century.

## Survival did not make the catastrophe bearable

There is a difficult ethical question hidden inside the discovery that your grandparents met because of the Holocaust. Their meeting may be part of the reason you exist, but that does not make the Holocaust an event for which you should feel grateful. Trying to balance one person's existence against an unspeakable horror is absurd because the two claims do not belong on the same moral scale.

This is close to the difficulty philosophers identify in the non-identity problem. If the alternative history would contain a different person instead of a happier version of the same person, our usual language of making somebody better or worse off begins to struggle.

Perhaps we should not try to have it both ways when it comes to the war's legacy. You can feel personally grateful to be alive without feeling grateful for the catastrophe that happened before you. “This event was necessary for me to exist” is not a statement of praise or moral debt. “This event was morally superior to the alternative” is a separate claim and, in the case of the war, an indefensible one.

That goes beyond World War II, and beyond war. Some people were born because political decisions, natural disasters, plagues, or pandemics changed the course of their families. They are descendants of survivors, people who built their lives atop the ruins of the old order. It is not enough to say that something terrible happened and some people are alive because of it. The lives these people lead now, the opportunities open to them, and the world they inhabit are qualitatively different from what could have followed otherwise. A baby boom is a wave of events that can be charted, but it is also a collection of individual timelines and circumstances that nobody could have predicted.

## The subtleties of counterfactual thinking

There is a certain kind of self-indulgent historical fiction that wants to do nothing but describe how lives could have been different if the protagonist had made five different choices at five different points in time. It can be entertaining, but it should also leave you feeling as if you have been tricked. This is why it is important to remember certain limits when discussing how a different train in Hayange could have changed the lives of the people on it.

We cannot calculate an exact proportion of people living today who owe their existence to World War II. We do not know what the alternative for any family would have been, and we should not assume that it was identical across Europe or elsewhere. We cannot say how significantly the timelines would have diverged in, say, Soviet Russia and America after the same event. Nor can we predict what other events may have coincided with the hypothetical change.

Above all, we must not confuse identity with heredity. The claim is not that the war somehow entered a person's DNA. It is simpler: the war changed survival, location, relationships, and timing. Those changes affected which people met and which gametes combined.

When it is stated in this way, it hardly seems remarkable, but that makes the events it describes no less horrifying. It is not necessarily a matter of government propaganda, but of opportunity. Something as simple as an order can take away a person's opportunity to have a child or make it impossible for them to meet a future spouse. It may be hard to think about the war as something that made us who we are in this way, but it is an exercise in humility to remember that a shift in the timing of one or two events can be enough to produce completely different descendants.

## What the photo does not say

The civilians in Hayange represent a moment captured in time. Beyond the frame are the people in charge of the trains, the schedules, the destinations, and the passengers' former homes. They might have returned to those places or gone somewhere entirely different. It is possible that a conversation on the train eventually led to a marriage. I would not attempt to write a story about who they were or what they had been through based on this picture. There is simply not enough information to form a cohesive narrative with a beginning, middle, and end.

That lack of information serves to highlight the point. There are countless personal timelines that were interrupted in ways we cannot document because nobody yet considered them worthy of documentation. A train ride became a transfer to another location, which meant that somebody saw somebody else for the first time. Years later, one of them—or one of their descendants—might have looked at a picture like this and wondered what it could have meant if it had been a different day.

We think of our birth as the beginning of our story, but biologically speaking it is the end of a long series of near misses. World War II intervened in that series for an enormous number of families. It left us with the dead, the survivors, and uncountable possible people who were never conceived. We memorialise the first group and keep records of the second. The third group is impossible to memorialise, which is why thinking about them changes the way we think about the war.
