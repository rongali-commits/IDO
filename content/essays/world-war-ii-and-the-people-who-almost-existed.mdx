In January 1945, an American Army photographer named W. Chichersky stood at the railway station in Hayange, France, as displaced Russian civilians were loaded onto a train. The surviving caption tells us where they were and what the soldiers were doing. It does not tell us what happened to the passengers afterwards.

Look at the photograph for a while and the word *displaced* begins to seem inadequate. A person can be displaced from a house, certainly, but also from a job, a courtship, a wedding date, a village in which everyone knows everyone else. War did this to millions of people. It broke the sequence in which their private lives had been proceeding and replaced it with another sequence that nobody had chosen.

That disruption has an implication I find difficult to hold alongside the scale of the suffering. World War II did not only determine who died. By altering who met, who returned, who waited, and when couples were reunited, it also affected which particular children came into existence. Many people alive now owe the exact timing of their conception to a chain of events in which the war appears somewhere upstream.

This is not a story about providence. It gives the war no credit. It is a story about how precarious a person's identity is.

## The unromantic biology of being one person rather than another

When parents conceive a child at a different time, they do not usually produce the same child with a revised birthday. A different egg may be available; almost certainly a different sperm reaches it. The result is another possible person.

That distinction sounds fussy until the time difference becomes large. Imagine a couple who planned to have a child in 1942. One partner is sent away and returns in 1946. If they have a baby after the reunion, nobody sensible would call that baby a delayed edition of the child they might have conceived four years earlier. The later child has a different genetic beginning. The first child was possible, then ceased to be.

The same logic applies at much smaller intervals. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of the non-identity problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nonidentity-problem/) describes the “precariousness” of existence: modest changes in the timing or manner of conception are enough to change the person conceived. The philosophical problem is normally used to examine our obligations to future people. Before reaching the ethics, though, there is a plain fact worth absorbing. Our family trees may look solid on paper, but the route to each name was extremely narrow.

War is unusually effective at pushing people off those routes.

## The railway timetable belongs in family history

Military history gives us operations, casualty figures, production totals, and diplomatic agreements. Family history is moved by smaller mechanisms: a cancelled train, an extra month in a camp, a letter that fails to arrive, an employer who transfers workers to another city.

Europe after the war was full of such interruptions. The United Nations describes millions of refugees scattered across the continent and hundreds of camps administered by the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration. These were not waiting rooms outside history. People worked, formed friendships, searched for relatives, married, separated, and had children in them. The [UN archive on wartime refugees](https://archives.un.org/en/content/wwii-refugees) includes the Tannenbaum family, Holocaust survivors living in an UNRRA camp in Grugliasco, Italy, where their son Peter had just been born. His birth was not an abstract demographic consequence. It happened in one camp, to one family, after a particular survival.

The point is not that every wartime displacement produced a new family. Many produced loneliness, permanent separation, or death. Nor can we take a person born after the war and identify the single event without which they would not exist. Causes spread and mingle too quickly for that. What we can say is that mass movement changed the occasions on which people encountered one another, and conception is acutely sensitive to occasion.

This makes the railway station in Hayange part of reproductive history, although no official caption would call it that.

## Survival does not turn catastrophe into a benefit

There is a moral trap here. Suppose someone learns that their grandparents met because both were displaced by the war. Without the displacement, that person would not exist. Does it follow that the war was good *for them*?

The question feels indecent because it tries to put existence and atrocity on the same scale. It also exposes why the non-identity problem is a problem. If the alternative to the war contains a different person rather than a happier version of the same person, then our usual language of being made better or worse off begins to slip.

We should resist the temptation to solve the discomfort with gratitude. Existing because of a catastrophe does not create a debt to the catastrophe. A person can be glad to be alive while condemning every coercion, invasion, murder, and deprivation in the chain that happened to precede them. “This event was necessary for me” and “this event was morally justified” are entirely different statements.

The distinction matters beyond war. Migration, illness, economic collapse, pandemics, and political decisions all redirect intimate lives. Some descendants will exist only because those disruptions occurred. Their existence does not retrospectively compensate the people who suffered through them.

## A baby boom is made of individual timings

The American baby boom is often presented as a broad curve: births rise after 1945, stay elevated, then fall. The curve is real, but it can conceal what it is counting.

The US Census Bureau defines baby boomers as people born from 1946 through 1964. It estimated that 76.4 million members of that cohort were living in the United States in 2012, a figure that included later immigration. The first sharp increase came in 1946, when births rose as service members returned and postponed marriages and households resumed. Housing policy, prosperity, social pressure, and changing expectations about family size all helped shape what followed.

Now take the graph apart. A man comes home in September rather than December. A couple marries after demobilisation instead of before enlistment. A woman who entered wartime employment decides against the marriage her family expected. One person moves into a new suburb and meets a neighbour. Each change is negligible to a national total. For the identity of a child, it may be everything.

This is why I am wary of saying that the war simply “created” the post-war generation. There is no clean line from battlefield to maternity ward, and millions of births would have occurred in a peaceful 1940s. The defensible claim is narrower: a different 1940s would have produced a substantially different set of people, even where the total number of births eventually looked similar.

## The counterfactual has limits

It is easy to become intoxicated by alternate history. Once every train and meeting acquires reproductive significance, a writer can invent anything and hide behind the fact that the other timeline cannot be checked.

So the limits need to remain visible. We cannot calculate the percentage of people alive today whose existence depends on World War II. We cannot assume the effect was equal in a Soviet village, an American suburb, a South Indian town, and a Pacific island. We cannot know how quickly two possible histories would diverge, because changing one event changes the conditions for everything after it.

We also should not mistake identity for heredity. The claim is not that the war somehow entered our DNA or that trauma is genetically carried by every descendant. The relevant mechanism is more ordinary: the war altered survival, location, partnership, and timing, which altered which gametes combined.

Once stated that way, the idea becomes less sensational and more unsettling. A government order need not touch a chromosome to decide which chromosomes ever meet.

## What the photograph cannot show

The civilians at Hayange occupy one fraction of one second in the archive. Beyond the frame are officials, timetables, destinations, previous homes, and futures the photographer could not know. Perhaps some passengers returned to families. Perhaps others settled elsewhere. Perhaps a conversation on that journey eventually led to a marriage. Any confident story about them would be an invention, so I will not supply one.

Still, the absence of a story is part of the point. History contains countless private consequences that no archive recorded because they did not yet look like consequences. A delay became a meeting; a meeting became a household; decades later, somebody opened an old photograph and recognised nothing of themselves in it, even though their existence might run through the scene.

We tend to imagine our birth as the beginning of our story. Biologically, it was the successful end of a long sequence of near misses. World War II violently rearranged that sequence for an enormous number of families. It left us both the dead, whose absence can be named, and possible people whose absence cannot.

The second group has no memorial. It cannot. But thinking about it changes the meaning of the people who are here.
