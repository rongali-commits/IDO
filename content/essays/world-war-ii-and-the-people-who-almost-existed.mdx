Japanese aircraft attacked Visakhapatnam, South India, in 1942. I was born in a village nearby decades later, and that fact led me to a strange thought that the war might be the reason I exist now.

Recently, when I learned that the war had reached Visakhapatnam, I began to wonder whether I would have been born if the war hadn’t happened.

A single ejaculation contains millions of sperm, and they don’t carry the same genetic combination. The egg also depends on the reproductive cycle in which conception takes place. Intercourse can result in conception across a fertile window lasting several days. When a couple has sex can alter which sperm reaches which egg or prevent conception altogether. Research into the [timing of intercourse and ovulation](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199512073332301) shows how tightly conception is tied to a short run of days.

Take the same couple and two pregnancies: one in 1939 and the other in 1945. The child conceived in 1939 can’t wait six years and reappear as the child conceived in 1945. This idea sits close to what philosophers call the [non-identity problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nonidentity-problem/). A changed decision might not leave the same future person better or worse off. It might replace that person with somebody else. War does this on a scale that’s hard to picture. It changes who dies, where people travel, and countless ordinary decisions, including when couples conceive children. The world population in the 1930s was over two billion people. The United Nations places the [two-billion mark around 1927](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n11/213/43/pdf/n1121343.pdf). Within little more than a decade, an enormous share of those people had been drawn into a war that rearranged daily life across much of the world. Close to 70 million people died, depending on which indirect deaths an estimate includes. Every death closed off possible relationships and births. Survivors married different partners or didn’t marry at all. Children who grew up without parents might have moved to a different town, taken up unfamiliar work, or met a different person they would eventually marry. Soldiers, refugees, and civilians moved within countries and across borders. The [United Nations’ history of forced displacement](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4005894) describes how tens of millions of people migrated to different places during the war. Some couples never saw each other again. Each birth depended on its own chain of circumstances.

## Small events can do the work

A train being delayed during an air raid might be enough, as might a poor harvest or a soldier returning in 1946 instead of 1945. When the children born from these altered circumstances grow up, they meet partners who belong to the altered world, and their children do the same. After several generations, there is no realistic way to reconstruct the family line that would have existed without the first disruption.

## Could anyone have escaped the ripple?

Complete immunity would demand something extraordinary: every conception between 1939 and the birth of a person living today would have to occur with the same parents despite the war’s absence. We can’t prove that for anyone. Even so, some communities may have been more immune than others. Historically isolated peoples in the Amazon, the Sentinelese in the Indian Ocean, and some communities in New Guinea probably experienced fewer immediate disruptions than families living under mass mobilisation and occupation. An isolated farming village offers a similar possibility. If traders in a nearby town changed their route because of shortages, a village calendar might change too. People conceived before the war form an exception. Their initial genetic identity was already there, but their survival and later lives were not. Once they had children in the postwar world, the insulation weakened as their descendants chose partners amid new borders and unfamiliar economies. One generation might stand outside the question, but the next usually steps back into it.

## The war reached my home

At first, I thought I might have been born with or without World War II, just because I was born in a small remote area in South India, but my assumption was wrong. Visakhapatnam wasn’t safely removed from the conflict.

The Greater Visakhapatnam Municipal Corporation records that [Japanese aircraft attacked the city in 1942](https://www.gvmc.gov.in/static_content/History.jsp). At the same time, close to half a million Indians fled Burma as Japanese forces advanced, and colonial relief efforts extended into [Visakhapatnam district](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/beyond-indenture/after-the-long-march-colonialera-relief-for-burma-indian-evacuees-in-visakhapatnam-district-19421948/3A450247E040B73F55BA13B03BDD99E8). For a question that began with events supposedly far away, that’s a local connection. Britain also bought huge quantities of war material inside India. Military spending changed labour demand and agricultural production; research on [wartime mobilisation and India’s economy](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33246) traces the size of that shift. What I have is a region where population movements were all being disturbed at once, which makes it impossible to rule out the butterfly effect on me. I can’t look at this history and sensibly claim that my family line floated above it untouched. You can apply the same logic to your existence.

World War II might still have happened even without Hitler. The Treaty of Versailles, the Great Depression, militarism, antisemitism, and fascist movements had made Europe dangerous before Hitler took power. Removing him leaves those forces in place, but Hitler still wasn’t interchangeable with any other German politician. His pursuit of *Lebensraum* in Eastern Europe, joined to his racial ideology, shaped the territories Germany attacked and the kind of war it waged there. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum explains how [Nazi racial thinking tied conquest to the removal and murder of whole populations](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/nazi-racism). Without Hitler, Europe could still have gone to war. It wouldn’t necessarily have gone to war in September 1939, against the same countries, or with the same plans for extermination. For my argument, those differences in timing and direction matter as much as the question of whether war occurs at all.


## The failed painter’s real irony

Hitler tried to impose a racial order on future generations through conquest and enslavement. He wanted to decide who was entitled to have descendants, but the war he started produced movements beyond anyone’s ability to plan. His effort to control the future created the population he couldn’t have imagined.

That’s the irony... Hitler was rejected by the Academy of Fine Arts Vienna in 1907 and failed to gain admission again, a part of his early life recorded by the [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/adolf-hitler-early-years-1889-1921). Had the academy accepted him, he might have followed another path. Art school wasn’t a magic switch that controlled the century. Acceptance wouldn’t automatically have erased his prejudices, political ambitions, or the forces pushing Europe toward conflict. The story matters as a symbol of contingency.
If some event had stopped him from becoming dictator or even delayed the European war, the population alive now might contain different individuals. Perhaps its total size would be similar. Perhaps not.


Looking back at our own family’s history, it’s hard to avoid once you start thinking about it. The war left the dead and the survivors. It also left people who were never conceived because history unfolded differently than it might have.
